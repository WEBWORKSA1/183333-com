The 183-Day Rule, Explained Without the Myths

By the 183333 editorial teamReviewed 2026-10-269 min read

What the 183-day rule really is, where it comes from, how days are counted, and why staying under 183 days does not automatically keep you out of a country's tax net.

Ask ten remote workers how tax residency works and nine will say the same thing: "stay under 183 days and you're fine." It is the most repeated rule of thumb in international living — and one of the most misunderstood. This guide explains what the number actually does, where it comes from, and the traps that catch people who treat it as a magic shield.

Why 183?

183 is simply the first whole number that is more than half of a 365-day year. A person who spends 183 days somewhere has spent the majority of the year there, which is a reasonable, objective signal that the place is their main base. Legislators like it because it is easy to verify from passport stamps, travel records and, increasingly, digital entry/exit systems.

But "majority of the year" is only a signal. Most modern tax systems combine a day count with questions about where your home is, where your family lives, where your work and income are, and where your life is centred. The day count is often the easiest test to fail — and rarely the only one.

The two different jobs the number does

People blend two separate rules that both happen to use 183 days:

  1. Domestic residency tests. Each country writes its own law on who counts as a resident. Many use 183 days in a calendar year (Spain, Ireland, Singapore), some use any 12-month period (Portugal, Greece, Estonia, the UAE), some use 180 or 182 (Thailand, India, Malaysia), and some have no day count at all (Mexico, the Netherlands). Meet the domestic test and the country can usually tax your worldwide income.
  2. Treaty rules on employment income. The OECD Model Tax Convention — the template behind most double-tax treaties — contains a separate 183-day condition in the article on employment income. Broadly, if you are resident in Country A and work in Country B, B can generally only tax your salary if you are present there for more than 183 days in a 12-month period, or your employer is a B resident, or the cost is borne by a B branch. This rule decides which country taxes a salary; it does not make you a resident.
Key takeaway: staying under 183 days in a country can keep its domestic day test from triggering, but other residency tests (home, family, economic ties) can still apply — and the treaty salary rule has two more conditions that have nothing to do with days.

How a "day" is counted

The single biggest source of error is counting. Rules differ on:

  • Partial days. Many countries count any part of a day as a full day. Arrive at 23:30 and leave at 00:30 two days later and you may have spent three "days". The UK counts a day if you are present at midnight, with limited transit exceptions.
  • The measuring window. Calendar year, fiscal year (the UK runs 6 April to 5 April; Australia 1 July to 30 June; India 1 April to 31 March) or a rolling 12-month period that can straddle two tax years.
  • Exclusions. Some regimes ignore days you were stuck because of a medical emergency, days in transit between two foreign points, or days as a student or diplomat. Exclusions are narrow and usually must be claimed with evidence.
  • Weighting. The US Substantial Presence Test looks back three years and weights earlier years at one-third and one-sixth. 122 days a year, every year, crosses the US line.

Five myths that cost people money

1. "Under 183 days everywhere means I pay tax nowhere."

Your home country usually keeps taxing you until you formally break residency, and many countries (Canada, Australia, the UK through its ties test) look at your connections, not just days. The US taxes citizens wherever they live.

2. "183 days resets on 1 January."

Only for calendar-year tests. Rolling-window countries look at any 12 consecutive months, so a stay from July to June can trigger residency even though neither calendar year passes 183.

3. "Leaving the country resets the clock."

Days are cumulative within the window. A visa run does not reset a tax day count, and in the Schengen Area it does not reset the 90/180 count either.

4. "A digital nomad visa settles my tax position."

A visa is an immigration permission. Some nomad permits come with tax relief (Croatia, for example, exempts qualifying foreign employment income), but many do not, and none of them stop your home country from taxing you.

5. "If two countries both claim me, I pay twice."

Not necessarily. Where a treaty exists, tie-breaker rules allocate residency — see our tie-breaker guide. Where no treaty exists, foreign tax credits may still reduce double taxation, but you will usually need professional help.

The 183·3·3·3 method

Our name is built around a simple discipline for anyone living across borders:

  • 183 — know the day threshold and the window in every country you spend time in.
  • 3 years — keep a three-year look-back, because the US and several other systems use multi-year formulas, and audits commonly reach back that far or further.
  • 3 tests — for every country, check days, home and centre of interests, not just days.
  • 3 proofs — keep three independent records for every stay: travel documents, payment or accommodation records, and a contemporaneous log.
