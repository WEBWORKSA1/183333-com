Resident in Two Countries? How Treaty Tie-Breakers Decide

By the 183333 editorial teamReviewed 2026-10-268 min read

How the OECD-model tie-breaker ladder (permanent home, vital interests, habitual abode, nationality) resolves dual residency, and what to document.

Because every country writes its own residency law, it is entirely possible to be resident in two countries at once — for example, resident in Portugal under its 183-day rule and still resident in Canada because of your home and family ties. When a tax treaty exists between the two, its residency article usually contains a tie-breaker ladder that assigns you to one country for treaty purposes.

The tie-breaker ladder

Most treaties follow the OECD Model. You go down the ladder only until one step gives an answer:

  1. Permanent home available to you. A home (owned or rented) continuously available — not a hotel or short stay. If you have one in only one country, that country wins.
  2. Centre of vital interests. If you have permanent homes in both (or neither), where are your personal and economic relations closer? Family, social life, occupation, business, assets, and where you manage your property all matter.
  3. Habitual abode. If vital interests cannot be determined, where do you stay more often over a meaningful period? Day counts come back in here.
  4. Nationality. If still unresolved, the country of which you are a national.
  5. Mutual agreement. Finally, the two tax authorities settle it between them.
Notice that the day count only appears at step 3. Someone who keeps a permanent home and family in one country can lose the tie-breaker there even after spending 200 days elsewhere.

What the tie-breaker does — and does not — do

  • It decides residency for treaty purposes, which then controls which country has primary taxing rights over most income.
  • It does not cancel filing obligations. Some countries still expect a return or a disclosure claiming treaty residence elsewhere.
  • US citizens are a special case: the "saving clause" in US treaties preserves US taxation of its citizens.
  • Some newer treaties (influenced by the multilateral instrument) replace the ladder with case-by-case agreement between authorities.

What to document

  • Lease or deed for every home, and evidence of whether it was available year-round.
  • Where your spouse/partner and children lived and went to school.
  • Employment contracts, client locations, and where you actually performed work.
  • Bank accounts, investments, memberships, doctors, driving licence and vehicle registrations.
  • A reliable day log — use the Day Tracker and export it each month.

Employment income: the treaty 183-day rule

Separately, the employment article typically lets only your country of residence tax your salary for work done abroad if all three apply: you are present in the other country no more than 183 days in a 12-month period (or tax year, depending on the treaty), your employer is not resident there, and no permanent establishment there bears your pay. Fail any one and the work country may tax the salary for days worked there.
